Chapter 01

Introduction to Modern Hardware

Computer hardware has undergone more transformation in the last five years than in the previous two decades combined. The convergence of AI workloads, energy constraints, and the physical limits of silicon scaling has forced engineers to completely rethink how chips are designed, fabricated, and packaged.

From the first transistors etched at micron scale to today's 2-nanometre silicon with billions of transistors on a fingernail-sized die, the story of computer hardware is one of relentless ingenuity — driven by the insatiable demand for more performance, lower power, and smaller form factors.

History of Innovation

Key Milestones

1971
Intel 4004 — First Commercial Microprocessor
Intel's 4004 put 2,300 transistors on a single chip at 10µm node — beginning the era of programmable computing hardware. Clock speed: 740 kHz.
1993
Intel Pentium — Superscalar Architecture
The Pentium introduced dual-pipeline superscalar execution, allowing two instructions per clock cycle. 3.1M transistors at 0.8µm. Consumer PCs became viable workstations.
2003
AMD Athlon 64 — 64-bit Computing Goes Mainstream
AMD beat Intel to 64-bit x86 desktop computing, bringing the ability to address more than 4GB of RAM to consumer systems and transforming server workloads.
2012
NVIDIA Kepler — GPU General-Purpose Computing
Kepler's CUDA architecture enabled GPUs to accelerate scientific, financial, and machine learning workloads — planting the seed for the AI hardware era.
2017
Google TPU v2 — Purpose-Built AI Silicon
Google's second-generation Tensor Processing Unit demonstrated that custom ASIC accelerators could outperform GPUs for specific AI tasks, triggering an industry-wide AI chip race.
2020
Apple M1 — ARM Conquers the Desktop
Apple's unified memory architecture M1 chip proved that ARM-based CPUs could match or beat x86 in performance while consuming a fraction of the power — reshaping the laptop industry.
2023
NVIDIA H100 — The AI Infrastructure Standard
Built on TSMC N4 with 80 billion transistors, the H100 became the de-facto compute unit for training large language models — sold out globally throughout 2023 and 2024.
2025
Blackwell, Turin, M4 — The Current Generation
NVIDIA Blackwell, AMD EPYC Turin (192 cores), Apple M4 Max, and IBM's 1,121-qubit Condor define the frontier of 2025 — with 2nm silicon, HBM4, and the first steps toward fault-tolerant quantum computing.
Context

Why Hardware Matters Now

🤖
AI Demands It
Training GPT-4 scale models required ~25,000 A100 GPUs running for months. Next-generation AI requires hardware breakthroughs — not just more chips.
🌍
Energy Crisis
Data centres now consume ~2% of global electricity. Energy efficiency is no longer optional — it's the primary engineering constraint of the decade.
🏭
Geopolitics of Silicon
Semiconductor supply chains are now a national security issue. TSMC, ASML, and advanced packaging facilities are geopolitical assets as much as industrial ones.
